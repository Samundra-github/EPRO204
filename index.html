Working with Children, Families, and Communities in Contemporary Society

This professional digital portfolio supports Australian early childhood educators working with children aged birth to five years. Grounded in sociological theory, contemporary research, and national and South Australian policy frameworks, it examines evidence-based practice across two essential contexts: supporting immigrant and refugee families, and supporting children and families affected by natural disasters and climate-related emergencies.

A

Section A – Understanding the Context

Cultural and linguistic diversity is central to contemporary Australian early childhood education and care (ECEC). The 2021 Census demonstrates that 27.6% of Australians were born overseas, 48.2% have at least one overseas-born parent, and 22.2% speak a language other than English at home (Australian Bureau of Statistics [ABS], 2022). Concurrently, Australia’s Humanitarian Program resettled 20,000 refugees and humanitarian entrants in 2023–24, many of whom are young families fleeing conflict in Afghanistan, Myanmar, Syria, and East Africa (Department of Home Affairs, 2024). According to the Australian Early Development Census (AEDC), 26.8% of children (81,885 children) enter school with a Language Background Other Than English (LBOTE), up from 18.0% in 2009 (Department of Education, Skills and Employment [DESE], 2022). Because early childhood services are often the first Australian institution families encounter, educators play a decisive role in fostering belonging, cultural safety, and settlement recovery (Lamb, 2020).

Sociological theories guide educators to move beyond tokenistic multiculturalism into responsive practice. In Bronfenbrenner’s (1979; Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2007) Bioecological Systems Theory, a child’s classroom development (microsystem) is directly shaped by educator–parent communication (mesosystem), parental visa conditions and settlement services (exosystem), national migration attitudes (macrosystem), and pre-migration displacement across time (chronosystem). For example, when a newly arrived 3-year-old shows separation distress, an educator strengthens the mesosystem by engaging a bilingual worker during unhurried drop-offs rather than viewing the child through an attachment-deficit lens. Bourdieu’s (1986) Cultural Capital Theory explains how monolingual Anglo-Australian routines marginalise migrant families when their home languages and oral traditions are devalued; educators counter this by embedding children’s home languages (L1) into daily group times as valued academic capital (McInnes et al., 2024). Finally, Social Constructionism (Berger & Luckmann, 1966; James & Prout, 1997) shows that developmental norms such as solo sleeping or independent self-feeding are culturally constructed, prompting educators to respect collectivist caregiving practices and treat immigrant children as capable social agents.

B

Section B – Impact on Children and Families

Migration and forced displacement significantly influence young children’s development, wellbeing, cultural identity, and learning participation. Refugee children exposed to pre-migration conflict, unstable transit, or parental distress frequently display pre-verbal trauma responses in early childhood settings—including hypervigilance, acute startle reactions to loud noises or alarms, sleep difficulties, and withdrawal from peer play (Berger et al., 2023; Lamb, 2020). Simultaneously, emergent bilingual children navigating an English-dominant room often experience a receptive "silent period." While AEDC data show that LBOTE children still acquiring English at school entry have higher vulnerability in the communication domain, bilingual LBOTE children who are supported to maintain their first language alongside English demonstrate strong social competence and emotional maturity (DESE, 2022). When services enforce subtractive English-only expectations, children risk cultural shame and erosion of home-language fluency with parents and elders (de Heer et al., 2016; McInnes et al., 2024).

Families simultaneously face complex settlement stressors: navigating unfamiliar bureaucracy, housing insecurity, underemployment, loss of extended kinship networks, and language barriers—with 852,706 Australians speaking English "not well" or "not at all" (ABS, 2022). Complex online Child Care Subsidy (CCS) applications and English-only enrolment forms can deter family participation, while refugee survivors of state persecution may feel apprehension toward official authorities (Due & Riggs, 2016; Lamb, 2020). For early childhood educators, this requires replacing transactional paperwork with trauma-informed relational pedagogy: using accredited interpreters, extending orientation periods, establishing predictable visual routines, and actively representing families’ cultures to uphold identity and belonging (Australian Government Department of Education [AGDE], 2022).

Section C – Social Policy and Australian Responses

1. National: EYLF V2.0 & National Quality Standard (QA1 & QA6)

Purpose: Belonging, Being and Becoming (V2.0) (AGDE, 2022) and the NQS (Australian Children’s Education and Care Quality Authority [ACECQA], n.d.) mandate Respect for Diversity, Cultural Responsiveness, and family partnerships, supporting home languages (Outcome 5) and cultural identity (Outcome 1).

Evaluation & Practice: Guides daily anti-bias curriculum and family co-design; however, without mandatory trauma training, some services default to tokenistic cultural celebrations rather than deep linguistic inclusion (McInnes et al., 2024).

Official EYLF V2.0 Document

2. National: Inclusion Support Program (ISP)

Purpose: Administered by the Australian Government Department of Education (AGDE, 2023) to build ECEC capacity to include children from culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) and refugee backgrounds via Strategic Inclusion Plans and Bicultural Support.

Evaluation & Practice: Funds bicultural workers to translate intake meetings and support orientation, though strict time caps and application complexity limit ongoing bilingual staffing after initial settling.

Official ISP Policy Portal

3. South Australia: Preschool Bilingual Support Program & Multicultural Charter

Purpose: Operationalises the South Australian Multicultural Charter (Department of the Premier and Cabinet South Australia, 2023) by placing Bilingual Support Workers in SA Department for Education preschools (Department for Education South Australia, 2023a).

Evaluation & Practice: Highly effective in helping multilingual preschoolers use their first language as a bridge to learning, though allocated hours are rationed and rarely cover private long-day care centres.

Section D – Strategies for Practice (Birth to 5 Years)

Strategy 1: Translanguaging, Visual Scaffolding & Home-Language Story Baskets

1. What it involves: Integrating children’s home languages (L1), real-photo visual lanyards, and family-recorded audio stories/lullabies into daily routines and play spaces (EYLF V2.0 Outcomes 1 & 5).

2. Research support: South Australian research by McInnes et al. (2024) proves that valuing children’s home languages strengthens social-emotional security, peer inclusion, and English acquisition while validating family cultural capital (Bourdieu, 1986).

3. Implementation: During orientation, educators partner with parents and bilingual workers to record phonetic comfort words and songs, pair them with visual schedule magnets, and build tactile Dual-Language Story Baskets.

4. Classroom example: For a 14-month-old Arabic-speaking toddler, the educator uses photo cue cards for meals and nappy changes while playing the mother’s recorded Arabic lullaby at sleep time. In preschool (3–5 years), a 4-year-old Hazaragi-speaking child uses a story basket to teach peers greetings and counting in Hazaragi.

5. Expected benefits: Reduces anxiety during the silent period, affirms cultural identity, and builds anti-bias peer empathy.

Strategy 2: Trauma-Informed Primary Caregiving & Sensory Sanctuary Spaces

1. What it involves: Assigning a consistent Primary Caregiver, staggered unhurried orientations, gentle transition cues, and a low-stimulation "Cosy Sanctuary Nook" for self-regulation (EYLF V2.0 Outcome 3; NQS QA2 & QA5).

2. Research support: Lamb (2020) and Berger et al. (2023) demonstrate that refugee children experiencing pre-migration or settlement trauma require predictable relational safety to lower cortisol hyperarousal before exploration can occur.

3. Implementation: One key educator greets the child and parent daily, replaces loud bells with soft chimes and 5-minute visual warnings, and furnishes a sanctuary tent with familiar cultural textiles, family photos, and weighted lap cushions.

4. Classroom example: A 3-year-old from a humanitarian background who startles at sudden noises holds a scarf carrying his mother’s scent, reviews a custom photo routine book ("Mum goes to English class, I play, Mum returns after fruit"), and receives calm one-on-one co-regulation in the sanctuary nook prior to evacuation drills.

5. Expected benefits: Builds secure secondary attachment, lowers physiological hypervigilance, and deepens family trust in the service.

A

Section A – Understanding the Context

Australia faces escalating frequency and severity of climate-related natural disasters, including bushfires, riverine and flash floods, droughts, and extreme heatwaves (Marinkovic Chavez et al., 2024). During the 2019–2020 Black Summer bushfires, over 24 million hectares burned and 3,000 homes were destroyed (Royal Commission into National Natural Disaster Arrangements, 2020), followed by major eastern Australian floods and the 2022–2023 South Australian River Murray floods, which inundated 91,000 hectares and damaged thousands of homes (Government of South Australia, 2023). Nationally, over 1.4 million Australian children are exposed to a natural disaster in an average year (Royal Far West & UNICEF Australia, 2021). Although children aged birth to five are among the most physiologically and emotionally vulnerable groups during emergencies, their developmental needs are frequently overlooked in adult-centric disaster planning (Emerging Minds, 2023; Gibbs et al., 2015).

Sociological frameworks highlight the vital protective role of early childhood services before, during, and after crises. Through Bronfenbrenner’s (1979; Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2007) Bioecological Systems Theory, a disaster is an acute chronosystem shock that simultaneously fractures the child’s microsystem (damaged homes, evacuated preschools, lost pets) and exosystem (lost parental livelihoods, closed health clinics); restoring a predictable ECEC microsystem buffers the young child when the home environment is under strain. Social Capital Theory (Aldrich, 2012; Putnam, 2000) demonstrates that community recovery depends on bonding capital (close family attachment), bridging capital (horizontal neighbourhood solidarity where the preschool acts as a trusted community hub), and linking capital (connecting families with Recovery SA, government relief, and mental health services). Finally, Socio-Ecological Resilience Theory (Masten, 2001, 2014; Ungar, 2011) conceptualises resilience not as an innate personality trait, but as "ordinary magic" generated when everyday protective systems—warm educator co-regulation, predictable routines, and therapeutic play—shield the child’s developing stress-response system.

B

Section B – Impact on Children and Families

Natural disasters affect infants, toddlers, and preschoolers across physical, neurodevelopmental, emotional, and educational domains. Biologically, young children under five are highly susceptible to bushfire smoke particulate matter, extreme heat, and floodwater contaminants (Royal Far West & UNICEF Australia, 2021). Psychologically, because birth-to-5 children cannot regulate intense autonomic arousal independently, trauma manifests somatically and behaviourally: heightened separation anxiety, sleep and toileting regression, hypervigilance to weather triggers (wind, thunder, smoke, or sirens), and repetitive disaster re-enactment in play (Emerging Minds, 2023). Longitudinal evidence from the Australian Beyond Bushfires study (Gibbs et al., 2015; Gibbs et al., 2019) reveals that early childhood disaster exposure can produce delayed declines in primary school reading and numeracy years later if early developmental disruptions are not buffered.

A young child’s recovery is closely tied to caregiver wellbeing. Families affected by bushfires or floods often experience housing displacement, financial loss, and elevated parental post-traumatic stress, which can temporarily reduce parents’ capacity for attuned emotional co-regulation (Gibbs et al., 2015; Marinkovic Chavez et al., 2024). Simultaneously, emergency closures of early learning centres disrupt children’s routines precisely when stability is most needed, while early childhood educators often navigate dual trauma as both disaster survivors and frontline caregivers (Berger et al., 2022; Berger et al., 2023). Consequently, services must provide psychological first aid, flexible enrolment and fee relief for displaced families, educator wellbeing support, and a predictable "safe base" classroom environment.

C

Section C – Social Policy and Australian Responses

1. National: Regulation 97 & NQS Quality Area 2 (Element 2.2.2)

Purpose: Legally requires all approved ECEC services to conduct annual disaster risk assessments, maintain Emergency Management Plans, and rehearse evacuation/lockdown procedures every 3 months (ACECQA, n.d.).

Evaluation & Practice: Ensures strong physical safety compliance, though audits often emphasise procedural speed over trauma sensitivity; educators must scaffold quarterly drills playfully using social stories so young children are not frightened.

Official NQF Guide (ACECQA)

2. National: Disaster Mental Health Framework & CCS Emergency Provisions

Purpose: The National Disaster Mental Health and Wellbeing Framework (National Emergency Management Agency [NEMA], 2023) prioritises infant and family recovery, paired with federal Child Care Subsidy (CCS) Period of Emergency fee waivers and allowable absences.

Evaluation & Practice: CCS gap-fee waivers keep displaced children connected to their centre during financial hardship, though community mental health funding often tapers off before delayed child trauma resolves (Royal Far West & UNICEF Australia, 2021).

Official NEMA Portal

3. South Australia: High Bushfire Risk Register & State Emergency Plan

Purpose: Classifies high bushfire-risk SA preschools and kindergartens, mandating annual readiness audits, Bushfire Refuge buildings, automatic closures on Catastrophic Fire Danger days, and recovery coordination via Recovery SA (Department for Education South Australia, 2023b; Department of the Premier and Cabinet South Australia, n.d.).

Evaluation & Practice: Pre-emptive closure on Catastrophic days protects lives, while shaping annual spring audits, infant comfort grab-bags, and family emergency communication trees in SA centres.

SA High Bushfire Risk Policy

Section D – Strategies for Practice (Birth to 5 Years)

Strategy 1: Therapeutic Story-Sharing & Restorative Play Provocations

1. What it involves: Pairing therapeutic disaster picture books (such as Birdie’s Tree) and hand puppets with open-ended small-world, water/sand, and regeneration play provocations (EYLF V2.0 Outcomes 2 & 3; NQS QA1 & QA5).

2. Research support: Emerging Minds (2023), Berger et al. (2023), and Masten (2014) show that under-5s process frightening events symbolically through play; educator-scaffolded helper and regrowth play shifts children from helplessness to mastery.

3. Implementation: Educators read Birdie and the Fire with Birdie and Mr Frog puppets, then provide play trays with blocks, native animal figurines, vet kits, and SA Country Fire Service (CFS)/SES helpers alongside seed-planting stations.

4. Classroom example: After local flooding, an educator sits beside toddlers scooping water with toy rescue boats, calmly narrating safety ("The helper boat brought all the calves to dry land"). Preschoolers build an animal hospital and plant fast-sprouting native seeds to observe green shoots returning.

5. Expected benefits: Enables safe externalisation of big feelings, restores agency, and nurtures environmental hope without eco-anxiety.

Strategy 2: Embodied Somatic Co-Regulation & Playful Emergency Readiness

1. What it involves: Embedding daily somatic breathing and movement routines into the schedule alongside trauma-informed, play-based quarterly evacuation rehearsals (Regulation 97; EYLF V2.0 Outcomes 1 & 3; NQS QA2).

2. Research support: Gibbs et al. (2015) and Emerging Minds (2023) establish that predictable routines and physiological co-regulation calm amygdala hyperarousal triggered by wind, smoke, thunder, or alarms.

3. Implementation: Educators use a daily visual "Weather & Feelings Check-In," practice playful somatic games ("Turtle Breath" and "Cooling the Hot Soup"), and introduce quarterly evacuation drills using a custom photo social story and comfort grab-bags.

4. Classroom example: When strong winds rattle a South Australian preschool, the educator dims harsh lights, leads three rounds of "Turtle Breath" and humming on the mat with weighted lap cushions, and reassures children: "The wind is outside; our roof and teachers keep us safe inside."

5. Expected benefits: Prevents re-traumatisation during mandatory drills, builds embodied self-soothing skills, and models co-regulation for parents.
